#### Exploring Sexual Violence in Vietnam

**What is sexual violence?**

Sexual violence refers to harmful behaviours of a sexual nature carried out against a person's will. It can be perpetrated by a current or former partner, other people known to the person, family members or strangers. People of all genders and ages can be victims of sexual violence.

Examples of sexual violence include:

- Rape and other forms of sexual harm within marriage or dating relationships
- Rape by strangers or acquaintances
- Unwanted sexual advances or sexual harassment (at school, at home, at work etc.)
- Coercion (to be explained in Module 2)
- Rape and sexual abuse of children
- Technology facilitated violence

#### Can Sexual Violence Occur Within Relationships?

Yes, any kind of violence that occurs within the context of an intimate relationship (married, engaged, dating, casual hook-ups) is defined as intimate partner violence.

There's a lot of terminology when talking about sexual violence. Listed below are some other key terms you might come across. As you complete the course, you can also refer to the downloadable glossary of key terms (see [Glossary of Key Terms](#glossary-of-key-terms) above).

**Intimate partner violence** is any behaviour by a current or former partner or spouse that causes physical, sexual or psychological harm (WHO, 2024; UN Women, 2025).

Related to intimate partner violence, **dating violence** is defined as "the display of behaviours by couples towards each other, including verbal, sexual, emotional and physical violence in dating relationships, and imposing social restrictions on each other's lives" (Yilmaz and Taplak, 2021). As with all forms of violence, dating violence can occur in real life and online.

#### Is Sexual Violence Always Physical?

No. Sexual violence can include harmful behaviours that take place online and/or using technology.

**Technology-facilitated violence** refers to any act that is committed, assisted, aggravated or amplified using information communication technologies or other digital tools, that results in or is likely to cause harm (UN Women, 2022).

Some examples of harmful behaviours considered to be technology-facilitated violence include:

- Sending unwanted requests for nude photos or videos, or sending unrequested nude photos and videos of yourself
- Capturing and/or sharing sexual images or videos of someone else without the consent of that person and the recipient (sometimes called 'revenge porn')
- Spying on, monitoring or stalking someone through any type of surveillance device (such as a tracking device or spycam)

#### Sexual Violence in Vietnam

In Vietnam, one in three women (32%) experience physical and/or sexual violence in their lifetime. More than one in ten women (13.3%) experience sexual violence in their lifetime. Young women are most at risk of sexual violence (MOLISA, 2020).

However, Vietnamese men also experience sexual violence, with a 2020 study finding that verbal and physical violence is common from wives towards husbands, though usually at lower rates than husband-to-wife violence (ISDS, 2020).

##### Quick Reference Guide — Statistics on Types of Sexual Violence in Vietnam

**Intimate-partner violence**
- 2 in 3 married women (62.9%) in Vietnam experience violence by their husbands/partners in their lifetime.

**Dating violence**
- A study with 1,809 female Vietnamese students found that 64% had experienced dating violence (UN Women, 2023).

**Technology-facilitated sexual violence**
- Globally, the overall prevalence of online violence against women is 85%, and 88% in the Asia Pacific region (Economist Intelligence Unit, 2021).
- Another study based on 5 countries in the Asia region showed that online violence against women is very common (UN Women, 2020).
- Many instances of technology-facilitated sexual violence are not reported, so it is unclear exactly how common this form of violence is (Bansal et al., 2023).

**Sexual harassment**
- Verbal sexual harassment — i.e., making sexually suggestive comments or jokes that make others feel uncomfortable — is the most common form of sexual harassment.
- A study involving students and lecturers from Hanoi University of Education, Hong Duc University and Thai Nguyen University of Education revealed that 51.8% of students and 30.2% of staff and lecturers had experienced sexual harassment during a school year (UN Women, 2023).

#### What Is Consent?

Consent means choosing to say 'yes' to something. For example, if someone asks to borrow your phone, you can say 'yes' and hand it over, or you can say 'no' and keep it in your pocket.

In sex and relationships, consent means giving or refusing permission for sexual activity. Consent is a form of communication – through words, actions or both. Any sexual activity without consent is sexual violence.

#### Why Is Consent Important? / Who Is Responsible for Consent?

**Why is consent important?**

Consent keeps people safe, physically and emotionally. Healthy relationships include clear communication and respect for each other's choices. Safe sex between consenting adults can be a positive experience.

Consent is always necessary – whether you are just dating, in a long-term relationship, or married. Being in a relationship does not mean automatic consent. Everyone has the right to decide what happens to their body. Silence is not consent. Consent must be clear and ongoing.

**Who is responsible for consent?**

Everybody is responsible for consent, regardless of gender. If you start sexual activity, it is your responsibility to ask for consent. If you do not want sexual activity, it is your responsibility to say 'no' or show it through body language. Your partner must respect your words and actions.

#### Legal Age of Consent in Vietnam

Many countries have laws about the 'age of consent', meaning the age at which someone is considered legally able to agree to sexual activity. In Vietnam, this age is unclear, as childhood ends at 16, but adulthood begins at 18. Vietnam has numerous laws about forms of sexual violence, including the Penal Code (2015) which criminalises domestic violence, workplace sexual harassment, sexual abuse and human trafficking.

Sexual activity should only take place between people who are old enough to understand what they are doing and freely agree to it. If one person in the relationship is older, this does not mean that their wants or needs matter more than the other's. Every person must consent every time sexual activity takes place.

Consent is not always as simple as the difference between legal and illegal, or 'yes' and 'no'. In many situations young people face, there is not a clear 'no' or an enthusiastic 'yes'. This is because saying 'yes' or 'no' to sex can be complicated by social norms and power dynamics. It is common for young people to have experiences of unwanted sex – that is, sexual experiences that they did not want. #### Coercion and Consent

The video you just watched contains some elements of sexual coercion, for example, when her boyfriend repeatedly asked and messaged Nguyen for sex, putting pressure on her to say 'yes'.

Sexual coercion can make you feel like you have no choice, but you can always say 'no', regardless of your gender or age. If you are pressured into sex, it is not true consent, and it is a form of sexual violence. You never have to do anything you don't want to.

Sexual coercion is when someone pressures, tricks or threatens you into sexual activity without using physical force. For example:

- Someone repeatedly asking for sex until you give in
- Lying or making false promises to convince you to have sex
- Threatening to break up or spread rumours unless you have sex
- Using a position of power (e.g., boss, teacher) to convince you to have sex

#### Consent and the Law (in Some Countries)

In many countries, there are laws that say sexual activity must be consensual. Non-consensual sex is a punishable offence in many places. More and more governments are introducing laws to help prevent sexual violence – including, among other forms, technology-facilitated sexual violence and coercive behaviours.

Practising consent not only aligns with values that are important to Vietnamese society, it also prepares you for global citizenship in places where you might choose to work or study. Around the world, workplaces increasingly require new employees to complete training in workplace safety – including setting clear expectations relating to respectful behaviour and sexual harassment laws.

#### Non-Consensual Filming and Sextortion

As we learned in Module 1, taking pictures or filming somebody without their knowledge is an example of technology-facilitated sexual violence. In some countries it is illegal. Unfortunately, this kind of sexual violence is common among young people in Vietnam.

If you want to take photos or videos of somebody in a sexual context, you must have their consent. If you photograph or film someone without their consent, you are doing them harm. This also applies to the sharing of images and video: the person shown must consent to the images/video being shared, including on what platform and with whom.

Similarly, you must have consent to show or share images or videos of someone in a sexual context. 'Sextortion' is a type of technology-facilitated sexual violence in which sexual images are used to blackmail a person.

### Module 2.2

#### The Communicative Approach to Sexual Consent

The communicative approach to consent is a way of applying consent to everyday life. It's a tool you can use in your sexual interactions with others. The communicative approach to consent is helpful because it emphasises that sexual consent is just as much about ensuring mutual enjoyment and safe sexual encounters, as it is about avoiding harm.

This approach to consent focuses on the following: the communicative approach to consent ensures yours and your partner's safety, through clear communication, in the pursuit of mutual enjoyment. Let's explore each of these below.

##### Safety

Sexual safety is important for everyone. Here are three simple tips to make sure consent is safe and respectful.

1. **Talk directly** – Tell your partner what you would like to do sexually and ask your partner what they want. Ask if there's anything important to know about their sexual history, and share what you feel comfortable with. Being direct, clear and honest helps keep both partners safe and comfortable.
2. **Prevent STIs** – Have a confidential conversation about sexual health. Ask if your partner has been tested for sexually transmitted infections (STIs). Some STIs have no symptoms, so regular testing is important. You can even suggest getting tested together.
3. **Plan for pregnancy prevention** – If pregnancy is possible and you want to avoid it, talk about contraception options and what you both prefer.

As you have these conversations, keep in mind that you and your partner might want different things. It is important to respect their responses. Consent is not about getting the answer you want, but listening to and respecting the answer you get.

##### Communication

Talking about consent is important in any sexual situation. You should always check if your partner feels safe and comfortable. Asking directly may feel awkward, but it's simple and necessary.

**Things you can say to check-in with your partner:**

- "Are you happy to do this?"
- "Is this okay?"
- "Do you mind if I hold your hand?"
- "Can I kiss you?"
- "Do you want to try ___?"
- "Want me to keep going?"
- "How are you feeling?"

Keep asking and checking in. It is OK for anyone to change their mind at any point during sexual activity. If you or your partner change your mind, you must stop immediately and respect one another's decisions.

Watch for non-verbal communication. People don't always say 'no' with words. Body language and facial expressions can also show discomfort. Select the heading below to reveal some of these non-verbal signs. If you see any of these, make sure you stop and ask if the person is OK.

**Non-verbal signs to look out for:**

- Tensing up
- Silence or not responding
- Crying or shaking
- Avoiding touch
- Not making eye contact

##### How to Say 'No'

It can be difficult to say 'no', but everyone is allowed to refuse sexual activity. You can change your mind at any time, no matter what has happened before. Select the heading below to learn some ways to say 'no' clearly.

**How to say 'no':**

- "No."
- "Can we stop?"
- "I don't want to do this anymore."
- "I thought I wanted to, but…"
- "I like you, but I can't do this right now."
- "I feel uncomfortable."
- "Let's watch a movie instead."
- "Let's wait until…"
- "I appreciate your company but I'm not ready."

Always ask, always listen, and always respect each other's choices.

##### Enjoyment

Sex should be enjoyable for everyone. The difference between enjoyable sex and sexual violence is that during enjoyable sex all participants feel safe, comfortable and respected. The communicative approach to consent can help achieve this.

##### Consent Under the Influence of Alcohol

Consent can be further complicated when alcohol or drugs are involved. For consent to be valid, both people must be sober enough to make a clear decision.

It's okay for people to drink alcohol and still give consent, as long as they are aware and able to make sound choices. But alcohol and drugs can affect the brain and make it harder to decide if you or someone else wants to have sex.

Also, research shows that excessive alcohol and drug use increases the risk of sexual violence (AHRC, 2017).

A person cannot give consent if they are too affected by drugs or alcohol. This might look like:

- Stumbling or needing support to stand
- Slurred speech
- Falling asleep or passing out
- Vomiting

If someone is heavily intoxicated, they cannot give consent. Having sex with someone who cannot consent is sexual violence.

#### Responding to a Friend

What do you do if a friend tells you they have experienced sexual violence?

Young people are far more likely to tell other young people – friends or peers – about an experience of sexual violence than they are to tell someone in authority or a health professional.

It can be confronting to hear that someone you know has experienced sexual violence. You might be upset by what they tell you, and it may also bring up memories of your own experiences.

If a friend chooses to share their experience of sexual violence with you, it's a brave decision on their part. They trust you enough to share something sensitive and difficult. This module will prepare you to respond in that situation.

It's OK not to have all the answers. Listening and being by their side is a great first step. You can also help them connect with expert services and resources, as and when they choose. Continue through this module to learn some other tips for being a responsible, caring friend.

##### Responding with LIVES

The World Health Organisation (WHO) uses the acronym 'LIVES', which you may also find useful as a model for responding to a friend who shares about an experience of sexual violence.

| Letter | Step | Example phrase |
|---|---|---|
| **L** | Listen with empathy and without judging | "I'm so sorry that happened to you. Thank you for trusting me enough to tell me about it." |
| **I** | Inquire about their needs and concerns | "What do you need right now? Is there anything you're especially worried about?" |
| **V** | Validate that you believe them and that they are not to blame | "You didn't deserve what happened to you. Sexual violence is never OK." |
| **E** | Enhance the person's safety | "Are you safe where you are? Do you need to see a doctor, or somewhere to stay for a while?" |
| **S** | Support by connecting them to information and services | "If it's OK with you, I am going to send you some information about support services for what you've experienced. Or, would you like me to ask on your behalf about what help is available to you?" |

##### How Not to Respond

Just as there are helpful ways to respond to someone sharing an experience of sexual violence, there are also unhelpful responses. One form of these damaging responses is sometimes called 'victim blaming'.

Victim blaming is any response that explicitly states or implies that the victim is to blame for the sexual violence they have experienced.

Victim blaming often focuses on actions the victim could have taken (or not taken) to avoid experiencing abuse. For example, victim blaming might sound like:

- "Why did you wear that outfit? No wonder they thought you wanted to have sex!"
- "How much alcohol did you have? I know what you're like when you're drinking..."

No one deserves to experience sexual violence. No matter the circumstances, sexual violence is always harmful and never the fault of the victim; the perpetrator is responsible for causing harm.

Avoiding victim blaming can help the person who has experienced sexual violence feel safer and more in control of their choices about how to move forward.

One of the best ways to care for someone who shares that they have experienced sexual violence is to connect them with support services where experts can assist them to make decisions and find help.
